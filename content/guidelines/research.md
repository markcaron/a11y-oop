# Inclusive research

## Start with the people who will use your experience

Accessibility work often begins with a list of requirements. Those requirements are useful, but they don’t tell us everything about the people using our products. Research helps us learn what people want to do, how they approach it, and where our experiences get in the way.

Include disabled people in your research. People who use screen readers, magnification, speech input, alternative keyboards, or other access strategies may encounter barriers that a standard usability session won’t reveal.

### Plan for participation

Before a session, ask participants what they need to take part comfortably. Share materials in accessible formats, check the research tools, and allow time for participants to use their own settings and equipment. Don’t assume that everyone with the same disability uses the same technology or approach.

Make invitations, consent materials and instructions understandable. Offer a way to discuss accommodations without asking participants to share more personal information than the research needs.

### Follow meaningful tasks

Choose tasks that matter to the people using your product. Record the user need, the task, the access strategy and the barriers you observe. Keep those task identifiers with your findings so designers, writers and engineers can follow the need through to verification.

A persona can help you ask a better question. It cannot tell you what a participant actually experienced. Label hypothetical, simulated and observed findings separately.

## Keep research and conformance connected

A usability study can reveal barriers and successful strategies. An expert review or automated test can identify different kinds of problems. Neither should be presented as a substitute for the other.

Bring these findings together when deciding what to improve. Keep the original observation, its context and the person responsible for following up.
